\section{A census of modal agents in the prisoner's dilemma}
\label{sec:modal-agent-census}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL: $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'', $q$ ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. We take the language
L of all formulas built from $p$, $q$, $\bot$, $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\Box$ with every atom
under a box, measure size as the number of nodes, and identify agents that behave identically
against every opponent. Payoffs are the standard 5 (temptation), 3 (mutual cooperation), 1
(mutual defection), 0 (sucker). An earlier finding on this site describes the self-defecting
families found in this census; this one reports the census itself.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Counts.} Up to size 9 there are 2815 behaviourally distinct agents (new per size 1 to 9: 1, 4, 8, 12, 31, 98, 255, 646, 1760), and every pairwise outcome is solved exactly.
\item \textbf{No evolutionarily stable agent.} Up to size 9, 180 of the 1335 observational classes are symmetric Nash equilibria, but none is an ESS. Every self-defecting equilibrium is invaded by FairBot, an argument that holds for every modal agent, not only those in the census. Under replicator dynamics from the uniform start, every surviving agent cooperates with itself and the mean payoff tends to 3.
\item \textbf{The round-robin winner never cooperates.} Up to size 9 the top score belongs to agents that act exactly like DefectBot. Up to size 11 the top 16 agents defect against everyone yet beat DefectBot, because more opponents cooperate with them (9863 against 9688); for example $\Box\Box p \wedge \neg\Box(p \vee \Box\bot)$.
\item \textbf{Self-defecting cooperators are almost always exploitable.} Among agents that defect against themselves but cooperate with FairBot and CooperateBot (2, 2, 4, \ldots{}, 2468, 7032 per size 4 to 14), only 3, 19 and 75 at sizes 12, 13 and 14 are unexploitable by every modal opponent. Each of the others is exploited by one of four simple agents: $\Box\neg p$, $\Box p$, $\Box q$ or $\Box\bot$.
\item \textbf{A threshold law, and where it breaks.} Up to size 14, every self-defecting agent that cooperates with FairBot cooperates with graded FairBots $F_k = \Box^k p$ exactly for $k \le a$ and with graded PrudentBots $P_i$ exactly for $i \ge b$, for some $a < b$. The law fails at size 17: the agent $Y = \Box(\Box\Box\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box(q \wedge \Box\Box\Box\bot))))$ cooperates with $F_1$ and $F_k$ for $k \ge 4$, but not with $F_2$ or $F_3$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The literature on modal agents studies a handful of named agents (FairBot, PrudentBot, CliqueBot
and a few more) \cite{barasz2014robust,lavictoire2014program}. A census makes the space itself
visible: it shows that program equilibrium among modal agents has no evolutionarily stable
point, that a round robin rewards agents that never cooperate, and that self-defecting
cooperators are rarely safe, with four tiny agents enough to expose all the others.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

Every outcome is computed exactly in the height semantics of closed GL fixed points (see
\cite{boolos1993logic}; the link to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem
\cite{solovay1976provability}) and re-checked on large samples by an independent solver; the
datasets are pinned by hash. The counts, equilibria and exploitability results hold for all
agents up to the stated sizes, not in general. The FairBot invasion argument and the
counterexample $Y$ hold in general.

\subsection*{Discussion}

The census is exhaustive only up to the stated sizes (9 for the counts and equilibria, 11 for
the round robin, 14 for exploitability and the threshold law), and only for the language L with
the single probe ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. Richer probes (how the opponent
treats FairBot or CooperateBot) would give a different, larger space, and the counts depend on
the exact choice of connectives and size measure. Results about tournaments and replicator
dynamics depend on the payoffs and on the uniform starting population; we report them as
properties of this population, not of modal agents in general. The FairBot invasion argument and
the counterexample $Y$ are the exceptions: they hold for all modal agents.

Two questions stay open. First, whether the unexploitable self-defecting agents found up to size
14 all belong to known families: some of them do, and the rest were shown different from family
members of small index but not classified. Second, whether the threshold law, which fails at
size 17, holds in a weaker form, for example for all agents of a given family. The natural next
searches are the same census for other two-action games (Chicken, Stag Hunt), where outcomes are
unchanged and only the scoring differs, and a census with more probes.

\section{When graded PrudentBots cooperate with graded FairBots}
\label{sec:graded-prudent-fair}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code. A
\emph{modal agent} \cite{barasz2014robust} decides by asking what Peano arithmetic (PA) can prove about
the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. Here $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ means ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot'', the agent
that always defects. FairBot, $\Box p$, cooperates exactly when it can prove that the opponent
cooperates with it. PrudentBot adds a safety test: it also requires a proof, in the stronger
system PA+1, that the opponent would not cooperate with DefectBot, so it does not reward an
opponent that cooperates with everyone.

We study two graded families. The graded FairBot $F_k = \Box^k p$ ($k \ge 1$) asks for $k$
nested layers of provability; $F_1$ is FairBot. The graded PrudentBot
$P_j = \Box p \wedge \Box(\neg\Box^j\bot \rightarrow \neg q)$ ($j \ge 0$) runs its safety test
in PA+$j$, since $\neg\Box^{j}\bot$ expresses the consistency of PA+$(j-1)$; $P_1$ is
PrudentBot.

\subsection*{Result}

$P_j$ and $F_k$ cooperate with each other exactly when $k \le j$; when $k > j$, both defect.
In words: a PrudentBot that runs its safety test in a stronger system can afford to cooperate
with FairBots that demand more nested proof, and the exchange rate is one for one. Every $P_j$
still defects against CooperateBot, so the extra cooperation costs nothing in safety.

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Barasz et al. remark that PrudentBot's safety test may use a stronger system than PA+1 and
that this widens the set of agents it cooperates with, and online discussion of the paper
looked at specific pairs of such variants. Here the relation is pinned down exactly for the
two graded families, for all indices: the boundary of cooperation is the single inequality
$k \le j$.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

Proved for all $j \ge 0$ and $k \ge 1$ in Lean 4, in the height semantics of closed GL fixed
points (on the chain of heights, both agents cooperate at every height when $k \le j$; when
$k > j$ both defect above height $j + k$). The link to provability in PA is Solovay's theorem
\cite{solovay1976provability} (see also \cite{boolos1993logic}), cited, not formalised, and the reading
in the standard model assumes PA is sound.

This result extends Barasz et al. (arXiv 1401.5577), remark after Theorem 3.2: PrudentBot's DefectBot check may use PA+n for larger n; LessWrong comments on ``Robust Cooperation in the Prisoner's Dilemma'' (orthonormal, cousin\_it): indexed Prudent(m,n)Bot variants with specific pairs. Our prior-art search (2026-10-21) covered:

\section{An unexploitable modal agent that defects against itself}
\label{sec:wn-gate-family}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved about the
opponent in Peano arithmetic, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. FairBot,
$\Box p$, cooperates exactly when it can prove that the opponent cooperates with it
($p$); CooperateBot always cooperates; DefectBot always defects. An agent is \emph{unexploitable} if
no modal opponent ever gets it to cooperate while the opponent defects. Graded FairBots are $F_k = \Box^k p$ and graded PrudentBots are
$P_j = \Box p \wedge \Box(\neg\Box^j\bot \to \neg q)$. We consider agents
built from $p$, from $q$ (``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''), $\bot$, $\neg$, $\wedge$,
$\vee$ and $\Box$, with every atom under a box, and measure size as the number of nodes in the
formula.

\subsection*{Result}

The agent $W = \Box(\Box\Box\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box q)))$ is unexploitable,
cooperates with FairBot and with CooperateBot, yet defects against an exact copy of itself. An
exhaustive search shows that no agent of size 11 or less has this combination of properties, and
$W$ (size 12) is one of the three smallest. It is the first member of an infinite family
$W_n = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box q)))$, $n \ge 2$, all with the same four
properties, with exact laws for how $W_n$ plays against graded FairBots $\Box^k p$, graded
PrudentBots and other members of the family.

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Every unexploitable agent in the literature we checked (FairBot, PrudentBot, CliqueBot)
cooperates with its own copy. $W_n$ shows that unexploitability, cooperation with FairBot and
generosity towards CooperateBot do not force self-cooperation: the delayed ``gate''
$\Box^n\bot$ lets the agent tolerate FairBot's failure to prove anything about DefectBot, while
two copies of $W_n$ end in mutual defection (both defect above height $n+1$).

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

The properties of $W_n$ and its laws against graded FairBots, graded PrudentBots and other
$W_m$ are proved for all indices in Lean 4 (height semantics of closed GL fixed points; the link
to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem \cite{solovay1976provability}, cited, not formalised; see also \cite{boolos1993logic}). The minimality of size
12 is an exhaustive computation over all agents up to size 12 in the stated language.

\section{Families of unexploitable agents that defect against themselves}
\label{sec:gate-families}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ means ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. FairBot is
$\Box p$, graded FairBots are $F_k = \Box^k p$, graded PrudentBots are
$P_j = \Box p \wedge \Box(\neg\Box^j\bot \to \neg q)$, and ConBot is $\neg\Box\bot$. An agent is
\emph{unexploitable} if no modal opponent ever gets it to cooperate while the opponent defects.

An earlier finding on this site described $W_n = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box q)))$: unexploitable, cooperative with FairBot and CooperateBot, yet defecting against
its own copy. This finding describes four further families of unexploitable agents that defect against
themselves, and how they relate.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gate families $V_{n,r} = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box^r q)))$}, which contain $W_n = V_{n,1}$. $V_{n,r}$ and $F_k$ cooperate exactly when $k + r \le n$; $V_{n,r}$ and $P_j$ exactly when $r < n < j$; any two members defect against each other. For $r < n$ every member is unexploitable, and these members are pairwise distinct.
\item \textbf{Prudent families $G_{m,j} = \Box(\Box^m p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$ and $H_{m,j} = \Box^{m+1}(p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$}, $m \ge j \ge 1$. Both are unexploitable, cooperate with FairBot and CooperateBot, defect against themselves, and cooperate with $F_k$ exactly when $k \le j$ and with $P_i$ exactly when $i > m$. So $G_{m,j}$ and $H_{m,j}$ cannot be told apart by graded FairBots or PrudentBots; the agent $S_j = \Box^{j+1}(p \vee \neg\Box^{j+1} q)$ separates them ($H_{m,j}$ defects against it while it cooperates; against $G_{m,j}$ both defect). ConBot separates the two kinds of family: $G_{m,j}$ defects against it, every $V_{n,r}$ with $n \ge 1$ does not.
\item \textbf{A common family $J_{a,b,j} = \Box^{a+1}(\Box^b p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$}, which contains $G$ ($a = 0$) and $H$ ($b = 0$). Checked for $a, b \le 5$, $j \le 6$: $J_{a,b,j}$ cooperates with $F_k$ exactly when $k \le j$ and with $P_i$ exactly when $i > a + b$, and exact laws hold against a two-parameter family of separating probes; the 70 members with $j \le a + b \le 5$ are pairwise distinct. The size-13 member $J_{1,1,1}$ has the same outcomes as $G_{2,1}$ and $H_{2,1}$ against $F_1, \dots, F_4$ and $P_0, \dots, P_5$, yet two opponents of size at most 9 distinguish all three.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The unexploitable agents we found in the literature (FairBot, PrudentBot and its graded and
probabilistic variants \cite{barasz2014robust}, \cite{oesterheld2022compatibility}) all cooperate with
their own copies. These families show that defecting against oneself is not a single odd
example: it comes in several infinite families with different mechanisms (a delayed gate on
the DefectBot test in $V$, a PrudentBot-style check in $G$, $H$ and $J$). Their behaviour
against graded FairBots and PrudentBots is described by simple thresholds, and these opponents
alone do not identify an agent: distinct members can agree on all of them and need other probes
to be told apart.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

The laws for $V_{n,r}$, $G_{m,j}$ and $H_{m,j}$ (including the separations by ConBot and $S_j$)
are proved for all indices in Lean 4, in the height semantics of closed GL fixed points; the link
to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem \cite{solovay1976provability}, cited, not
formalised (see also \cite{boolos1993logic}). The statements about $J_{a,b,j}$ and $J_{1,1,1}$ are
checked, not proved: exact tables for the stated parameter ranges, with every member of size at
most 15 independently certified by GL fixed-point solving. The unexploitability of $J_{a,b,j}$ is
supported by a short argument (the agent implies $\Box^{a+b+1}p$) and randomised safety tests,
not by a proof for all indices.

\section{A certified map of variants of Gödel's ontological argument}
\label{sec:ontological-variant-map}

\subsection*{Setting}

Gödel's ontological argument, in Scott's version, uses a second-order predicate $P$ (``positive'')
and five axioms: A1 (exactly one of a property and its negation is positive; split here into
A1a, ``not both'', and A1b, ``at least one''), A2 (positivity is closed under necessary entailment),
A3 ($P(G)$, where $G$, ``God-like'', means having every positive property), A4 (positive
properties are necessarily positive) and A5 (necessary existence is positive). The conclusion is
that a God-like being exists, $\exists x\,G(x)$, and necessarily exists, $\Box\exists x\,G(x)$.
A well-known side effect is \emph{modal collapse}: everything true is necessarily true.

Many variants and weakenings are studied, mostly one variant at a time with an automated
theorem prover \cite{benzmueller2025notes,benzmueller2026lean,benzmueller2020simplified,benzmueller2019ultrafilters}. This finding maps the space systematically. We take every subset
of each variant's axioms and the nine frame classes K, D, T, B, K4, KB, KD45, S4 and S5. We
vary the semantics: all intensional properties, rigid properties or extensional properties
(Fitting \cite{fitting2002types}), and constant or varying (actualist) domains. Cell by cell we
decide whether the axioms are consistent, whether they prove the conclusion and whether they
force collapse.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scott, all properties} (constant or varying domains): the God-like being exists exactly when the subset contains A1a, A2, A3 and A5 and the frames are B, KB or S5; necessary existence also holds on KD45. Collapse holds exactly when the subset contains A1a, A2 and A5, on the same frames. A1b and A4 are never needed. With A1, A2 and A5 alone, every symmetric frame is forced to consist of isolated worlds.
\item \textbf{Scott, rigid properties:} with constant domains no consistent cell forces collapse, and the conclusion holds on every frame exactly when the subset contains A1, A2 and A3, or A1, A3 and A5 (with Scott's essence). With varying domains existence needs A1a, A2, A3 and A5 on reflexive frames (T, B, S4, S5), or A4 as well on KB; with A4 added, necessary existence holds on every frame.
\item \textbf{Anderson's variant} (with $H = P(G_A)$): existence needs A1a, A2 and H on B, KB or S5, and on KD45 only with A4 or A1b, and only with constant domains. A5 follows from A2 and H. Adding A1b, the direction Anderson removed, brings collapse back on B and S5, and on KB only together with A4.
\item \textbf{Fitting's extensional variant} ($H = P(G)$, $N = P(NE)$): with constant domains no cell forces collapse, and the conclusion holds exactly for supersets of \{A1a, A2, H\} or \{A1a, H, N\}. With varying domains existence needs all of \{A1a, A2, H, N\} and reflexive frames (T, B, S4, S5); necessary existence also holds on KD45.
\item \textbf{Benzmüller's simplified variant} (no positive empty property, closure under entailment, closure under arbitrary meets): every cell is consistent and none collapses; the conclusion holds exactly for the full axiom set with closure under local entailment, and never with closure under necessary entailment alone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Each of these variants has been studied before, and many single cells of the map are known: the
dependence of collapse on symmetry, the sufficiency of KB, the redundancy of A5 in Anderson's
variant, the absence of collapse in Anderson's, Fitting's and the simplified variant
\cite{benzmueller2025notes,benzmueller2026lean,benzmueller2017hajek,benzmueller2020simplified}.
What we did not find is the complete picture: for each variant, exactly which axiom subsets and
frame conditions give the conclusion or collapse, and the minimal sets. The map shows, for
example, that in Scott's version neither A1b nor A4 ever matters, and that with rigid
properties and constant domains collapse disappears altogether.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

Every positive cell is proved in Lean 4 (core library, standard axioms only) in a shallow
embedding of higher-order modal logic on arbitrary Kripke frames of the class. Every negative
cell has an explicit finite countermodel, re-evaluated by a script. The tables cover the stated
axiom sets, semantic choices and nine frame classes; other variants (other definitions of
essence, other conjunction principles) are outside the map. We did not include the variants with
Gödel's binary conjunction axiom: that it fails on infinite domains is known
\cite{benzmueller2026ultrafilters}.

This result extends the single cells and per-proof axiom dependencies of Scott's and Gödel's variants established with other provers (Benzmüller \& Scott 2025; Benzmüller 2026); the analysis of Anderson's variant and the redundancy of its axioms (Benzmüller, Weber \& Woltzenlogel Paleo 2017); Fitting's and Anderson's collapse-free variants (Fitting 2002; Benzmüller \& Fuenmayor 2019); Benzmüller's simplified variant (2020). \subsection*{Discussion}

The map is a refinement of work that is largely known, not a new argument. Its value is
completeness: for each variant, every subset of the axioms and every listed frame class is decided
with a certificate, so the minimal sets and the frame boundaries can be read off rather than
inferred from separate studies. Several entries agree with results already established with
other provers \cite{benzmueller2025notes,benzmueller2026lean,benzmueller2017hajek}, which we take
as a check on our encoding rather than as findings.

The map covers only the stated choices: a shallow embedding of higher-order modal logic, Scott's
and Gödel's definitions of essence, the listed variants, nine frame classes and two kinds of
domain. Other definitions of essence, other readings of positivity (for example world-relative
and rigid mixtures), Gödel's conjunction axiom in all slices \cite{benzmueller2026ultrafilters}, and
logics beyond these frame classes are outside it. A cell decided here can therefore change under
a different encoding, and claims of minimality hold relative to the listed axioms.

What a reader can take away is where the strength of each variant sits: in Scott's version the
weight lies on A1a, A2, A3 and A5 together with symmetry, while A1b and A4 are idle; in
Anderson's version the dropped complement direction is exactly the switch for collapse; and in
the rigid and simplified readings, existence holds without collapse. Natural next steps are the
open cells the run left (mixed conjunction principles in some slices) and other definitions of
essence.

\section{Settling informal claims about modal agents in Chicken}
\label{sec:chicken-informal-claims}

\subsection*{Setting}

Modal agents play one-shot games by asking what can be proved about each other, written with the
provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL \cite{barasz2014robust}. Two informal posts made claims about
specific agents that were never checked. ``Modal Chicken'' \cite{gramstone2015chicken} introduced
CarefulBot and AstuteBot for the game of Chicken, and its comments proposed graded variants:
$C_n = \neg\Box(\Box^n\bot \vee p)$ (``swerve unless the opponent provably swerves'', with proof
strength $n$) and BoldBots $B_n = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee \neg p)$, the name a commenter gave to the
post's original CarefulBot ($B_0$); here $p$ means ``the opponent swerves against me''. ``Yet More Modal Combat'' \cite{hobson2021modal} proposed, for the prisoner's
dilemma, a bot that defects if a weak system proves the opponent cooperates and otherwise acts like
FairBot with a strong system; in graded form $H_{a,b} = \neg P_a(p) \wedge P_b(p)$ with
$P_a(z) = \Box(\Box^a\bot \vee z)$, $a < b$.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Graded CarefulBots and BoldBots, for all grades:} $C_n$ swerves against $C_m$ exactly when $n \le m$, while $B_n$ swerves against $B_m$ exactly when $n > m$. A CarefulBot against a BoldBot always goes straight while the BoldBot swerves, so the pair always ends at payoffs $(4, 1)$, whatever the grades.
\item \textbf{The named agents:} a certified table of all 64 pairings of SwerveBot, NerveBot, the original CarefulBot $B_0$, $C_0$, AstuteBot as displayed in the post, the FairBots $P_0(p) = \Box p$ and $P_1(p)$, and Hobson's bot $H_{0,1}$ (which cooperates with itself but ends in mutual defection with both FairBots). The original CarefulBot crashes against itself, as a comment pointed out; the displayed AstuteBot swerves against itself, as the post claimed.
\item \textbf{Hobson's bot, for all grades:} $H_{a,b}$ and $H_{c,d}$ end in mutual cooperation exactly when their weak grades agree ($a = c$), and otherwise in mutual defection. Against any FairBot of the form $P_e(p)$ it ends in mutual defection, for every $e$.
\end{itemize}

Informal claims about small agents are easy to make and easy to get wrong; two of the claims in
these posts were already disputed in their comment threads. These results settle them for every
grade at once. They also show that Hobson's bot, proposed as a bot that cooperates with FairBots,
does not cooperate with any graded FairBot of this form.

\section{Modal agents in the game of Chicken}
\label{sec:chicken-modal-agents}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot game and each can read the other's source code. Following Barasz et
al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved about the opponent,
written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. Earlier findings on this site study the
prisoner's dilemma; here the game is mostly \textbf{Chicken}: both swerve 3/3, swerve against straight
1/4, both go straight (crash) 0/0. $p$ means ``the opponent swerves against me'', $q$ ``the opponent
swerves against NerveBot'', the bot that never swerves. SwerveBot always swerves; FairBot is
$\Box p$, graded FairBots are $\Box^j p$. The census covers every agent built from $p$, $q$, $\bot$,
$\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\Box$ (atoms under a box) up to size 10, 7561 behaviourally distinct
agents. Mennen \cite{mennen2017modal} set up modal agents for general games; this finding works out
Chicken concretely.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Equilibria never crash.} Among the census agents there are 517 symmetric program equilibria, all with mutual swerving, and no equilibrium at all involves a crash. Every asymmetric equilibrium pairs one of the 701 agents that never swerve against anyone in the census with an agent that swerves against it.
\item \textbf{The smallest sensible Chicken agents} (swerve against themselves and against NerveBot, go straight against SwerveBot) have size 3: exactly $\neg\Box p$ and $\neg\Box q$. The first never crashes against any census agent; the second crashes against 755 of them.
\item \textbf{Safe and fair.} Exactly 4 census agents never crash against a census agent, go straight against SwerveBot and swerve together with FairBot, and all 4 never crash against any modal opponent at all. The smallest, of size 9, is the $k=2$ member of a family $Q_k = \Box^k\bot \vee \neg\Box(p \wedge q)$ with exact laws for every $k$: $Q_k$ never crashes, beats SwerveBot, swerves against itself, swerves together with $\Box^j p$ exactly when $j < k$ and with $\Box(\Box^j\bot \vee p)$ exactly when $j + 2 \le k$, and goes straight against $Q_m$ for $m > k$.
\item \textbf{A limit for every two-action game.} If an agent of modal depth $d$ exploits the always-cooperating bot, then graded FairBots $\Box^j p$ with $j \ge d$ end up playing action 2 against it, and the two never settle on mutual action 1. The $Q_k$ family shows the bound is tight.
\item \textbf{Tournaments and evolution.} In a round robin the top Chicken agent is $\neg\Box p \wedge \Box(\neg p \vee \Box p)$, which crashes against itself. Under replicator dynamics the surviving agents are the same 1116 classes in the prisoner's dilemma, Chicken and Stag Hunt, and they form a maximal set of mutual cooperators containing FairBot.
\end{itemize}

exploited?'' to ``can I avoid crashing without being a pushover?''. The census answers it
concretely: equilibria never crash, safe agents exist and form a family with exact thresholds, and
a general depth bound limits how such agents can coexist with graded FairBots.

\section{Three-player modal agents in a public-goods game}
\label{sec:three-player-modal-agents}

\subsection*{Setting}

Modal agents decide what to do by asking what can be proved about the other players, written with
the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL \cite{barasz2014robust}. Mennen \cite{mennen2017modal} set up a
framework for more than two players; here we study three players concretely. The game is a
public-goods game: each player contributes ($a_i = 1$) or not, and earns
$u_i = 2\sum_j a_j - 3a_i$, so contributing costs 1 net but gives each other player 2. An agent sees
$p_1, p_2$ (``opponent $j$ contributes in this game``) and probes $q_1, q_2$ (``opponent $j$
contributes when I am replaced by a defector``). Behaviour is read off in the long run (tail
semantics). An agent is \emph{safe} if it never earns less than either opponent, whoever they are.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The three-player FairBot is safe.} $F = \Box(p_1 \wedge p_2)$ (contribute iff it is provable that both others contribute) never earns less than either opponent against any two modal opponents, contributes with two copies of itself and defects against a defector.
\item \textbf{It is the smallest.} No agent smaller than $F$ (size 4) both cooperates with copies of itself and is safe.
\item \textbf{All small safe cooperators are FairBot-like.} Exactly 66 agents up to size 6 cooperate with themselves and are safe; none of them uses the probes, and for each GL proves that it contributes only if $\Box^3(p_1 \wedge p_2)$. Every other agent of that size fails against some pair of opponents of size at most 3.
\item \textbf{A prudent three-player agent needs the probe.} $E = \Box((p_1 \wedge p_2) \wedge (\Box\bot \vee \neg q_1))$ (size 10) cooperates with itself, is safe, and defects against two unconditional contributors; no agent of size at most 7 has all three properties.
\item \textbf{The definition of the probe matters.} For five basic three-player bots we certify all 125 triples. Changing the probe from ``only I am replaced by a defector'' to ``everyone except the queried opponent is replaced'' changes the outcome of 9 of the 125 triples.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Program equilibrium with modal agents has been worked out almost only for two players. In a
three-player public-goods game the natural FairBot generalisation stays safe, is the smallest safe
cooperator, and is essentially the only kind up to size 6. Exploiting unconditional contributors
requires the probe, as with PrudentBot in two-player games, and the exact definition of the probe
is a real modelling choice, since outcomes change with it.

computations over all agents up to the stated sizes in the stated language, with GL certificates.

\section{Blending self-defecting modal agents with FairBot: exact laws}
\label{sec:blend-window-laws}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL: $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. FairBot is
$\Box p$, graded FairBots are $F_k = \Box^k p$. An agent is \emph{unexploitable} if no modal opponent
ever ends up defecting while the agent cooperates. Outcomes are computed in the height semantics:
a game is a sequence of moves at heights $0, 1, 2, \dots$, and its result is the eventual move.

Earlier findings on this site describe infinite families of unexploitable agents that cooperate
with FairBot and CooperateBot but defect against their own copy: the gate families
$V_{n,r} = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box^r q)))$, with $W_n = V_{n,1}$, and
$J_{a,b,j} = \Box^{a+1}(\Box^b p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$. Here we ask what
happens when such an agent is \emph{blended} with another agent X: a formula $\sigma_S$ without atoms
picks out a set $S$ of heights (finite, or all but finitely many), and the blend plays like X at
the heights in $S$ and like the family member elsewhere. X is FairBot, a graded FairBot,
DefectBot or CooperateBot.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{FairBot-type blends.} For a graded-FairBot blend with finitely many selected heights: - a $W_n$ blend cooperates with itself exactly when $S$ meets $\{k, \dots, n-1\}$; - a $J_{a,b,j}$ blend exactly when $k \le j$ and $S$ contains all of $\{j, \dots, a+b\}$; - for $V_{n,r}$ the condition is a window determined by the blend's DefectBot trace, and it is not monotone: for $V_{3,2}$, selecting height 2 makes the blend cooperate with itself, while selecting heights 2 and 3 does not.
\end{itemize}

  All these blends stay unexploitable and keep cooperating with FairBot and CooperateBot.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{DefectBot blends.} Playing DefectBot at even one height rules out mutual cooperation with FairBot and with every $F_k$, because an agent ends in mutual cooperation with $F_k$ exactly when it cooperates at every height of that game. A DefectBot blend of $W_n$ still cooperates with itself exactly when $S$ is a non-empty subset of $\{0, \dots, n-1\}$; for $J_{a,b,j}$ never.
\item \textbf{CooperateBot blends} cooperate with themselves only when CooperateBot is played at all but finitely many heights, and then DefectBot exploits them.
\item \textbf{Not FairBot in disguise.} For finitely many selected heights, a single graded FairBot ($F_{j+1}$ for $J$, $F_{\max(1,n-r+1)}$ for $V$) behaves differently against the blend than against FairBot or any $\Box^m p$. A blend selecting all but finitely many heights behaves exactly like FairBot only in the trivial case (FairBot at every height from 1 on); otherwise an explicit opponent tells them apart.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Unexploitable agents that defect against themselves seem to sit far from FairBot, which
cooperates with itself. These laws say exactly how much FairBot behaviour, and at which heights,
is needed to restore self-cooperation without losing safety, and they give an infinite supply of
unexploitable self-cooperating agents that are provably not FairBot.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

All laws are proved for all indices in Lean 4, in the height semantics of closed GL fixed points;
the link to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem \cite{solovay1976provability}, cited, not
formalised (see also \cite{boolos1993logic}). Finite grids of cases were also checked independently
by GL fixed-point solving with certificates.

\section{How large must a program be to tell graded FairBots apart?}
\label{sec:graded-fairbot-separators}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL: $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. Graded FairBots
are $F_k = \Box^k p$ ($F_1$ is FairBot). They are all unexploitable and cooperate with themselves
and with each other, and a LessWrong discussion \cite{onlyonefairbot2026} lists them among the agents
that share these properties with FairBot without being FairBot. We take guarded formulas built
from $p$, $q$, $\bot$, $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\Box$ and measure size as the number of nodes. An
opponent \emph{separates} two agents if it gets a different outcome against them.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Smallest separators.} Every opponent that separates $F_k$ from $F_{k+1}$ has size at least $k+5$, and the only one of size $k+5$ is $S_k = \Box(\neg q \vee \Box^k\bot)$ (up to the order of the disjuncts): it cooperates with $F_k$ and mutually defects with $F_{k+1}$. Without $q$ the minimum is $k+6$, attained by $T_k = \neg\Box(\neg p \vee \Box^k\bot)$.
\item \textbf{Depth.} Every opponent of modal depth at most $k$ gets the same outcome against $F_k$ and $F_{k+1}$, so a separator needs depth at least $k+1$; $S_k$ and $T_k$ show this is tight.
\item \textbf{Ingredients.} An opponent with no negation, or with no $\wedge$/$\vee$, gets the same outcome against every graded FairBot; without $q$ the same holds for every boxed opponent and every opponent with at most one negation and at most one binary connective.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Graded FairBots look alike: each is unexploitable, cooperates with itself and with every other
graded FairBot. These results say exactly how complex a program must be to tell two neighbouring
ones apart, and that the cost grows linearly with the grade.

$k+6$ holds for all $k$.

\section{Unexploitable modal agents that cooperate with themselves: a census}
\label{sec:only-one-fairbot-census}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL: $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. FairBot is
$\Box p$: it cooperates exactly when it can prove that the opponent cooperates with it. An agent
is \emph{unexploitable} if no modal opponent ever ends up defecting while the agent cooperates, and
\emph{self-cooperating} if two copies cooperate. We take guarded formulas (every atom under a box)
built from $p$, $q$, $\bot$, $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\Box$, measure size as the number of
nodes, and call two agents behaviourally equal when they make the same moves against every
opponent. Agents using only $p$ are the rank-0 agents of Barasz et al.

A LessWrong post \cite{onlyonefairbot2026} asked whether every rank-0 agent that is unexploitable and
self-cooperating is FairBot. A comment there shows that the answer is yes up to modal depth 1 and
no from depth 2, with examples such as $\Box\Box p$, FairBot over PA+1 and $\Box^k p$.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Rank 0.} Among rank-0 agents of size at most 15 there are exactly 5031 classes that are unexploitable and self-cooperating (4374 of them also cooperate with FairBot and CooperateBot). Only $\Box p$ itself is FairBot; every other class is told apart from FairBot by an explicit opponent.
\item \textbf{With $q$.} Allowing $q$, up to size 14 there are 26449 such classes (16756 up to behaviour); 387 of them behave exactly like FairBot against every opponent, and each of the others is told apart from FairBot by an explicit opponent.
\item \textbf{Why some different formulas are FairBot.} Two agents behave identically against every opponent exactly when they have the same moves against DefectBot and agree on every input the opponent can actually produce: until the agent first cooperates, the opponent's move against it equals its move against DefectBot. So, for example, $\Box(p \vee (q \wedge \Box\bot))$ is behaviourally FairBot.
\item \textbf{A small example that is not FairBot.} $\neg\Box\bot \wedge \Box(p \vee \Box\bot)$ (size 9) is unexploitable and cooperates with itself and with CooperateBot, yet mutually defects with FairBot; it is the agent WaitFairBot$_1$ of Barasz et al. Every smaller rank-0 agent that is unexploitable and self-cooperating cooperates with FairBot.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The LessWrong question was answered with a few examples. These counts make the answer
quantitative: among small agents, FairBot is one of thousands of unexploitable self-cooperators,
and for each one there is a concrete opponent that tells it apart from FairBot. The criterion for
behavioural equality explains which syntactically different agents are nevertheless FairBot.

\section{Round robins of modal agents in the Stag Hunt}
\label{sec:staghunt-round-robin}

\subsection*{Setting}

In the Stag Hunt both players do best by hunting stag together; hunting stag alone pays nothing,
and hare is a safe middle option. We write the payoffs as stag/stag $a$, stag against hare $s$,
hare against stag $t$, hare/hare $d$, with $a > t > d > s$. The players are modal agents in the
sense of Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}: programs that decide by what can be proved about the
opponent, with $p$ = ``the opponent hunts stag against me'' and $q$ = ``the opponent hunts stag
against HareBot''. FairBot $\Box p$ hunts stag exactly when it can prove the opponent does. We take
all 7,561 behaviourally distinct guarded agents of size at most 10 and let every agent play every
agent, itself included, with equal weight (a round robin).

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{FairBot never wins.} Normalising $a = 1$, $s = 0$, the triangle of strict Stag Hunts $0 < d < t < 1$ splits into exactly six regions, each won by a single agent, and FairBot wins none of them. The agent $A_3 = \Box(p \vee \Box^3\neg(p \vee q))$ wins on 44.6\% of the area, including every game with payoffs $(4, 0, t, 2)$ for $2 < t < 4$ and the game $(3, 0, 2, 1)$. At $(4, 0, 3, 2)$ it scores 23,411 against FairBot's 21,719.
\item \textbf{Why it beats FairBot.} $A_3$ reaches stag/stag with 4,444 of the 4,459 agents that hunt stag against StagBot and with none of the others; it gains on the first group and loses on the second, for a net gain of 1,692 points over FairBot at $(4, 0, 3, 2)$.
\item \textbf{Families behind the winners.} For every $k$, $A_k = \Box(p \vee \Box^k\neg(p \vee q))$ hunts stag against HareBot (and is left alone there), coordinates with StagBot, with every $A_m$ and with every $F_j = \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee p)$ (FairBot over a stronger system); the lenient FairBots $R_k = \Box(p \vee \Box^k p)$ are safe and coordinate with $\neg\Box^j\bot$ exactly when $j \le k$.
\item \textbf{The winner depends on the size bound.} In the same census the score of $R_k$ rises strictly with $k$ up to $k = 8$ and that of $A_k$ up to $k = 5$, so longer members of both families score higher than the census winners. The specific winners above are therefore artefacts of the size bound; what holds across the families is that lenient agents beat FairBot in this round robin.
\end{itemize}

often taken as the reference cooperative agent. In the Stag Hunt, where cooperating with a
defector costs less than in the prisoner's dilemma, agents that are more willing to hunt stag do
better than FairBot against a broad population, and exact payoff breakpoints show where each
agent takes over.

\section{Safe modal agents that coordinate in the Stag Hunt: size 13 is the minimum}
\label{sec:staghunt-safe-coordinators}

\subsection*{Setting}

In the Stag Hunt two players each choose stag or hare: both hunting stag pays most, hunting stag
alone pays least, and hare is a safe middle option. Here the players are programs that read each
other's source code. Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by
asking what can be proved about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the
logic GL: $p$ means ``the opponent hunts stag against me'' and $q$ ``the opponent hunts stag against
HareBot'', the agent that always hunts hare. Call an agent \emph{safe} if no modal opponent ever ends up
hunting hare while the agent hunts stag (the Stag Hunt reading of unexploitability). Two simple
probes test willingness to coordinate: $E_1 = \neg\Box\bot$, which hunts stag unconditionally in
practice, and $N_1 = \Box(\Box\bot \vee \neg q)$, which hunts stag against agents that do not

$\wedge$, $\vee$, $\Box$ and measure size as the number of nodes.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No small safe coordinator.} Among the 56,503 agent classes of size at most 12, exactly 356 reach stag/stag with themselves, with $E_1$ and with $N_1$, and every one of them is exploited by some opponent of size at most 10. \item \textbf{Size 13 is enough.} The agent $X_1 = \Box(p \vee \Box p) \wedge (\Box\bot \vee \neg\Box\neg p)$ of size 13 is safe against every modal opponent and reaches stag/stag with itself, with FairBot, with $E_1$ and with $N_1$. \section{Gödel's lemma L under the rigid reading, with no condition on positivity}
\label{sec:ontological-rigid-l}

\subsection*{Setting}

Gödel's lemma L says that a God-like being's property $G$ is positive, $P\,G$. In
Benzmüller's \emph{Proofs Without Nominals} \cite{benzmueller2026nominals} (v4, sec:rigid), the
conjunction axiom Ax1Gen is read \emph{rigidly}: it applies only to sets of properties given by
conditions formed in the object language $\mathcal L$, so Ax1GenR is a schema over
$\mathcal L$-conditions. v4 asks whether L follows under this reading with no condition on
positivity. The finding \emph{Nominal-free proofs} on this site gives a detector-certified proof in
v4's module \texttt{Ax1GenRigidVariant2}; this one proves the statement over v4's semantics.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{L under the rigid reading.} In every v4 $\mathcal L$-general model with nonempty individuals where the variant-2 axioms Ax2a, Ax2b, Ax4 and the instance of Ax1GenR for the set of globally positive properties $\{\psi \mid P\psi=\top\}$ hold at every world, $P\,G$ holds at every world. The proof is a derivation in the certified deep calculus of the companion finding, so it holds by that calculus's soundness theorem, with the deep axioms matched clause by clause to the Lean module.
\item \textbf{The schema reading is faithful.} In the full structure on any frame, actuality and positivity, if the rigid Ax1GenR holds for every set of properties then every deep instance $\mathrm{Ax1GenR}\,c$, for $c$ a rigid $\mathcal L$-condition (equations under connectives and carrier quantifiers), holds at every world.
\item \textbf{The instance is needed.} Ax2a, Ax2b and Ax4 alone do not give $P\,G$: in a full S5 model with three worlds and one entity, where $P\varphi$ holds iff $\varphi$ holds of the entity at two or more worlds, they hold while $P\,G$ and the Ax1GenR instance fail at every world.
\end{itemize}

from the axioms (\texttt{L\_R\_rigidpos}). The result here needs neither. The faithfulness lemma follows
the deep--shallow method of Benzmüller and Kirchner for other logics.

\section{From Lean proofs to certified derivations in the ontological embedding}
\label{sec:ontological-proof-translation}

\subsection*{Setting}

The companion findings on this site give v4 \cite{benzmueller2026nominals} a machine-checked
semantics: a deep proof calculus, sound and complete over $\mathcal L$-general models. Its
derivations were written by hand. The shallow embedding's Lean proofs are richer than
higher-order logic, which is how a nominal can hide in a proof that v4's detector passes. This
finding builds a Lean command that reads a proof and produces the certified derivation itself.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The translator.} For a theorem of a shallow v4 module whose proof stays within higher-order logic (introduction and elimination of the connectives and quantifiers, classical logic, \texttt{Eq.subst}, \texttt{congrArg}, \texttt{congrFun}, \texttt{funext}, \texttt{propext}, beta and eta, unfolding of the module's definitions), \texttt{\#deep\_translate X as N} reads the statement and the proof term and builds a derivation in the certified calculus, kernel-checked, whose conclusion is the deep image of the statement, matched by \texttt{rfl}. It also reads off the module's axioms the proof uses as an $\mathcal L$-theory $T$, and yields \texttt{N.valid}: the statement holds at every world of every v4 $\mathcal L$-general model of $T$ with nonempty individuals. A proof outside the fragment, or one violating the whole-term condition, is rejected with the first offending step named. The rejections are tests the build enforces: v4's own hybrid proof of \texttt{L\_R}, the detector-passing proof with a hidden nominal, and a minimal \texttt{congrFun} probe.
\item \textbf{Applied to v4's proofs.} L under the rigid reading (from the detector-certified proof of \texttt{L\_R}), unrestricted Ax1Gen from Ax1GenTwo in variants 2 and 3$'$, modal collapse, necessary and possible existence of a God-like being, and the essence theorems (\texttt{EssMemI}, \texttt{UniqueEss1}, \texttt{UniqueEss2}, \texttt{Ess\_iff\_Ess2}) in variant 2, 3$'$ and in v4's S4 module of variant 3, each now as a semantic theorem over $\mathcal L$-general models.
\item \textbf{The frame condition for Th3.} For Th3 ($\Diamond\exists^E G\to\Box\exists^E G$) from Ax2a, Ax2b and Ax3 in variant 2, translation gives validity on symmetric and on Euclidean frames. Reflexive and transitive frames do not suffice, nor does any set of frame formulas true on a fixed five-edge frame. The exact condition: Th3 holds everywhere in every $\mathcal L$-general model on $(W,R)$ iff no world outside an $R$-closed set $B$ of all-dead or all-serial worlds sees both a member and a non-member of $B$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

A detector certifies a proof only as far as the detector reads it correctly. The translator
replaces that trust with a kernel-checked derivation and the calculus's soundness theorem, and
it turns v4's Lean proofs into statements about v4's semantics without rewriting them by hand.
The Th3 result shows what the semantics adds: an exact frame condition, where the shallow
embedding only gives sufficient ones.

\section{Nominal-free proofs of the theorems Benzmüller's detector flags}
\label{sec:ontological-nominal-free-proofs}

\subsection*{Setting}

Benzmüller and Scott's \emph{Notes} prove variants of Gödel's ontological argument in a shallow
embedding of higher-order modal logic in classical higher-order logic \cite{benzmueller2025notes}.
Benzmüller's \emph{Proofs Without Nominals} \cite{benzmueller2026nominals} (v4) points out that the
embedding's quantifiers also range over \emph{nominals}, properties like $\lambda u.\,u = w$ that pick
out a single world. A proof that substitutes one proves a theorem of the embedding but not
necessarily of the modal object language $\mathcal L$. v4 ships a detector that flags such
``hybrid'' proofs in two modes (witness and whole-term), and lists the theorems of its Lean
modules it could only prove with a flagged step, among them two of its stated open questions:
whether the equivalence of the restricted conjunction axiom Ax1GenTwo with the unrestricted
Ax1Gen has a nominal-free proof in variants 2 and 3$'$, and whether Gödel's lemma L follows under
the rigid reading Ax1GenR with no condition on positivity.

\subsection*{Result}

For each flagged theorem below we give a new proof of the \emph{identical} statement, from the same
module's definitions, that v4's own detector passes in both modes (full names):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The equivalence question:} \texttt{Ax1Gen\_of\_Two} and \texttt{Two\_equivalent}, with \texttt{P\_of\_conj}, \texttt{loc\_of\_conj} and the related lemmas, in \texttt{Ax1GenTwoVariant2} and \texttt{Ax1GenTwoVariant3Plain} (logic K).
\item \textbf{The rigid question:} \texttt{L\_R} in \texttt{Ax1GenRigidVariant2}, from Ax2a, Ax2b and Ax4 only, with witness \texttt{PosGlobal} and no condition on positivity.
\item \textbf{Further flagged theorems:} modal collapse and Th5 in \texttt{Ax1GenBoxVariant3} (the reflexivity hypothesis unused); fourteen theorems of \texttt{GoedelVariantHOML3inS4oneFile} without \texttt{Rrefl} or \texttt{Rtrans}; the one-conjunct theorems of \texttt{Ax1GenOneVariant3}; collapse and the existence theorems of \texttt{Ax1GenTwoVariant3} and \texttt{Ax1GenTwoVariant3Th4}; \texttt{AllExist} and \texttt{MC\_OneWorld} of \texttt{Ax1GenTwoVariant3Plain}; and collapse in its possibilist and Anderson-quantifier versions.
\item \textbf{A model:} on two worlds with one always-actual entity and a universal frame, Ax1, Ax2a, Ax2b, Ax3, both forms of Ax4, Ax1Gen with its Two, One and Box restrictions, and Th4 hold, and necessary existence holds at both worlds, so \texttt{NoNecExist} fails there; and an abstract Henkin lemma describing what Ax1GenR forces over admitted rigid families.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{What is new}

v4 already has nominal-free proofs of two of the lemmas listed first (\texttt{ae\_Two} and
\texttt{G\_ex\_Two\_unconditional}, under those names), and frame-free \texttt{\_K} versions of most theorems of
\texttt{GoedelVariantHOML3inS4oneFile}; what this finding adds there are proofs under the original names
that the detector passes, and nominal-free routes to \texttt{EssSupp}, \texttt{EssDetermined} and \texttt{AllExist}.
The new results are the equivalence lemmas, which v4 proves only through the nominal arguments
\texttt{OneWorld} and \texttt{R\_Id} and lists as open, and \texttt{L\_R} with no condition on positivity, also listed
as open. v4's text and its Lean module disagree on whether the detector flags the witness of its
own \texttt{L\_R} proof; ours passes either way.

\subsection*{Why it matters}

v4 asks whether these results are theorems of the modal logic or only of its embedding. A
detector-certified proof answers that for every proof the detector reads correctly. The
companion findings on this site make the answer semantic: a complete calculus over v4's
$\mathcal L$-general models, the equivalence and L under the rigid reading proved in every such
model, and a hole in the detector that shows why the semantic proofs are needed.

\section{A complete calculus for the $\mathcal{L}$-general models of the ontological argument}
\label{sec:ontological-lgeneral-semantics}

\subsection*{Setting}

Benzmüller's \emph{Proofs Without Nominals} \cite{benzmueller2026nominals} (v4) judges proofs in the
shallow embedding of higher-order modal logic against $\mathcal L$-\emph{general models}: Henkin-style
models whose carriers are closed under the terms of the modal object language $\mathcal L$. Its
Proposition prop:lgeneral gives soundness over these models for proofs meeting a \emph{whole-term}
condition; its Example ex:separating gives a sentence of $\mathcal L$,
$\mathsf A\exists p.(\mathsf{Atom}\,p\wedge p)$, that the embedding proves but the condition
excludes, formalised in its propositional part only. v4 leaves open whether its weaker \emph{witness
criterion} alone implies the condition, and whether a separating model exists for any of its
axiom sets. This finding makes that semantics machine-checked.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A sound and complete calculus.} A deep natural-deduction calculus for the embedding's higher-order logic, with $\forall$E/$\exists$I instances restricted to $\mathcal L$-terms and Leibniz substitution at every type of $\mathcal L$, is sound over v4's $\mathcal L$-general models with nonempty individuals, and complete: for every $\mathcal L$-theory $T$ and sentence $\varphi$, $\varphi$ holds in all such models of $T$ exactly when the calculus derives it from $T\cup\{\exists x.\top\}$. Löwenheim--Skolem and compactness follow: for theories containing $\exists x.\top$, a failure has a countermodel with countably many worlds and countable carriers, and every consequence follows from a finite sub-theory.
\item \textbf{prop:lgeneral in deep form.} Leibniz with whole-term motives and $\mathcal L$-term sides derives the same sentences, and unrestricted Leibniz derives the separating sentence from any theory, so the motive clause is what keeps the calculus sound.
\item \textbf{ex:separating at all types.} v4's flip model (total frame on $2^{\mathbb N}$, one individual, empty positivity) is $\mathcal L$-general at every type of $\mathcal L$ and refutes the separating sentence at every world. A model of variant 2 (Ax1Gen, Ax2a, Ax2b, Ax3, Ax4), with modal collapse and a God-like being, also refutes it at every world: a separating model for one of v4's axiom sets. With finitely many worlds the sentence always holds, so such a model needs infinitely many.
\item \textbf{The equivalence, semantically.} In every $\mathcal L$-general model of variant 2 or 3$'$ (Ax2a, Ax2b, $P\,E$, Ax4, $\exists x.\top$), Ax1GenTwo holds everywhere exactly when unrestricted Ax1Gen does, and then modal collapse and a God-like being follow (in variant 2 with Ax3).
\item \textbf{The witness criterion does not suffice in practice.} A Lean proof of the separating sentence in v4's own \texttt{Ax1GenTwoVariant2} module, which uses the nominal $\lambda u.\,u=w$ only as an argument of \texttt{congrFun}, passes v4's \texttt{hybrid\_check.py} with no flags in both witness and whole-term mode. The sentence fails in the variant-2 model above, so the detector certifies a proof of a sentence that is not a consequence over $\mathcal L$-general models.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Prior work}

Soundness over $\mathcal L$-general models for whole-term proofs is v4's Proposition
prop:lgeneral, proved there in prose; here it is a Lean theorem about an explicit calculus. The
separating sentence is in substance the world-proposition axiom of Fine and Kaplan
\cite{fine1970propositional}: some true proposition entails every truth. Its failure in atomless
algebras is known for S5 with propositional quantifiers \cite{holliday2019note}, finite-support
(Fraenkel--Mostowski) constructions of Henkin models are standard \cite{gabbay2011nominal}, and
compactness and Löwenheim--Skolem for intensional general models follow Muskens's pattern
\cite{muskens2007intensional}. Existing completeness theorems for the embedding are relative to
Henkin models \cite{benzmueller2013quantified}, whose domains contain every singleton, so they are
not $\mathcal L$-general. We found no statement of completeness over $\mathcal L$-general
models, of a separating model for one of v4's axiom sets, or of the detector result.

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The first four items settle, inside Lean, the semantic question v4 states as open, and they
give the detector-certified proofs on this site a meaning: a whole-term proof is true in every
$\mathcal L$-general model. The last item shows that the detector's implementation can miss a
nominal hidden in a motive, which is the case v4's open question asks about. It does not refute
Proposition prop:lgeneral, which holds; it shows that certifying proofs with the detector is
not yet a substitute for the semantic proof.
