\section{An unexploitable modal agent that defects against itself}
\label{sec:wn-gate-family}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved about the
opponent in Peano arithmetic, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. FairBot,
$\Box p$, cooperates exactly when it can prove that the opponent cooperates with it
($p$); CooperateBot always cooperates; DefectBot always defects. An agent is \emph{unexploitable} if
no modal opponent ever gets it to cooperate while the opponent defects. We consider agents
built from $p$, from $q$ (``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''), $\bot$, $\neg$, $\wedge$,
$\vee$ and $\Box$, with every atom under a box, and measure size as the number of nodes in the
formula.

\subsection*{Result}

The agent $W = \Box(\Box\Box\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box q)))$ is unexploitable,
cooperates with FairBot and with CooperateBot, yet defects against an exact copy of itself. An
exhaustive search shows that no agent of size 11 or less has this combination of properties, and
$W$ (size 12) is one of the three smallest. It is the first member of an infinite family
$W_n = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box q)))$, $n \ge 2$, all with the same four
properties, with exact laws for how $W_n$ plays against graded FairBots $\Box^k p$, graded
PrudentBots and other members of the family.

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Every unexploitable agent in the literature we checked (FairBot, PrudentBot, CliqueBot)
cooperates with its own copy. $W_n$ shows that unexploitability, cooperation with FairBot and
generosity towards CooperateBot do not force self-cooperation: the delayed ``gate''
$\Box^n\bot$ lets the agent tolerate FairBot's failure to prove anything about DefectBot, while
two copies of $W_n$ never reach mutual cooperation.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

The properties of $W_n$ and its laws against graded FairBots, graded PrudentBots and other
$W_m$ are proved for all indices in Lean 4 (height semantics of closed GL fixed points; the link
to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem \cite{solovay1976provability}, cited, not formalised; see also \cite{boolos1993logic}). The minimality of size
12 is an exhaustive computation over all agents up to size 12 in the stated language.

\section{When graded PrudentBots cooperate with graded FairBots}
\label{sec:graded-prudent-fair}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code. A
\emph{modal agent} \cite{barasz2014robust} decides by asking what Peano arithmetic (PA) can prove about
the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. Here $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ means ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot'', the agent
that always defects. FairBot, $\Box p$, cooperates exactly when it can prove that the opponent
cooperates with it. PrudentBot adds a safety test: it also requires a proof, in the stronger
system PA+1, that the opponent would not cooperate with DefectBot, so it does not reward an
opponent that cooperates with everyone.

We study two graded families. The graded FairBot $F_k = \Box^k p$ ($k \ge 1$) asks for $k$
nested layers of provability; $F_1$ is FairBot. The graded PrudentBot
$P_j = \Box p \wedge \Box(\neg\Box^j\bot \rightarrow \neg q)$ ($j \ge 0$) runs its safety test
in PA+$j$, since $\neg\Box^{j}\bot$ expresses the consistency of PA+$(j-1)$; $P_1$ is
PrudentBot.

\subsection*{Result}

$P_j$ and $F_k$ cooperate with each other exactly when $k \le j$; when $k > j$, both defect.
In words: a PrudentBot that runs its safety test in a stronger system can afford to cooperate
with FairBots that demand more nested proof, and the exchange rate is one for one. Every $P_j$
still defects against CooperateBot, so the extra cooperation costs nothing in safety.

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Barasz et al. remark that PrudentBot's safety test may use a stronger system than PA+1 and
that this widens the set of agents it cooperates with, and online discussion of the paper
looked at specific pairs of such variants. Here the relation is pinned down exactly for the
two graded families, for all indices: the boundary of cooperation is the single inequality
$k \le j$.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

Proved for all $j \ge 0$ and $k \ge 1$ in Lean 4, in the height semantics of closed GL fixed
points (on the chain of heights, both agents cooperate at every height when $k \le j$; when
$k > j$ both defect above height $j + k$). The link to provability in PA is Solovay's theorem
\cite{solovay1976provability} (see also \cite{boolos1993logic}), cited, not formalised, and the reading
in the standard model assumes PA is sound.

\section{Families of unexploitable agents that defect against themselves}
\label{sec:gate-families}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'' and $q$ means ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. FairBot is
$\Box p$, graded FairBots are $F_k = \Box^k p$, graded PrudentBots are
$P_j = \Box p \wedge \Box(\neg\Box^j\bot \to \neg q)$, and ConBot is $\neg\Box\bot$. An agent is
\emph{unexploitable} if no modal opponent ever gets it to cooperate while the opponent defects.

An earlier finding on this site described $W_n = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box q)))$: unexploitable, cooperative with FairBot and CooperateBot, yet defecting against
its own copy. This finding describes four further families of unexploitable agents that defect against
themselves, and how they relate.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Gate families $V_{n,r} = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box^r q)))$}, which contain $W_n = V_{n,1}$. $V_{n,r}$ and $F_k$ cooperate exactly when $k + r \le n$; $V_{n,r}$ and $P_j$ exactly when $r < n < j$; any two members defect against each other. For $r < n$ every member is unexploitable, and these members are pairwise distinct.
\item \textbf{Prudent families $G_{m,j} = \Box(\Box^m p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$ and $H_{m,j} = \Box^{m+1}(p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$}, $m \ge j \ge 1$. Both are unexploitable, cooperate with FairBot and CooperateBot, defect against themselves, and cooperate with $F_k$ exactly when $k \le j$ and with $P_i$ exactly when $i > m$. So $G_{m,j}$ and $H_{m,j}$ cannot be told apart by graded FairBots or PrudentBots; the agent $S_j = \Box^{j+1}(p \vee \neg\Box^{j+1} q)$ separates them ($H_{m,j}$ defects against it while it cooperates; against $G_{m,j}$ both defect). ConBot separates the two kinds of family: $G_{m,j}$ defects against it, every $V_{n,r}$ with $n \ge 1$ does not.
\item \textbf{A common family $J_{a,b,j} = \Box^{a+1}(\Box^b p \wedge (q \vee \Box(\Box^j\bot \vee \neg q)))$}, which contains $G$ ($a = 0$) and $H$ ($b = 0$). Checked for $a, b \le 5$, $j \le 6$: $J_{a,b,j}$ cooperates with $F_k$ exactly when $k \le j$ and with $P_i$ exactly when $i > a + b$, and exact laws hold against a two-parameter family of separating probes; the 70 members with $j \le a + b \le 5$ are pairwise distinct. The size-13 member $J_{1,1,1}$ has the same outcomes as $G_{2,1}$ and $H_{2,1}$ against $F_1, \dots, F_4$ and $P_0, \dots, P_5$, yet two opponents of size at most 9 distinguish all three.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The unexploitable agents we found in the literature (FairBot, PrudentBot and its graded and
probabilistic variants \cite{barasz2014robust}, \cite{oesterheld2022compatibility}) all cooperate with
their own copies. These families show that defecting against oneself is not a single odd
example: it comes in several infinite families with different mechanisms (a delayed gate on
the DefectBot test in $V$, a PrudentBot-style check in $G$, $H$ and $J$). Their behaviour
against graded FairBots and PrudentBots is described by simple thresholds, and these opponents
alone do not identify an agent: distinct members can agree on all of them and need other probes
to be told apart.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

The laws for $V_{n,r}$, $G_{m,j}$ and $H_{m,j}$ (including the separations by ConBot and $S_j$)
are proved for all indices in Lean 4, in the height semantics of closed GL fixed points; the link
to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem \cite{solovay1976provability}, cited, not
formalised (see also \cite{boolos1993logic}). The statements about $J_{a,b,j}$ and $J_{1,1,1}$ are
checked, not proved: exact tables for the stated parameter ranges, with every member of size at
most 15 independently certified by GL fixed-point solving. The unexploitability of $J_{a,b,j}$ is
supported by a short argument (the agent implies $\Box^{a+b+1}p$) and randomised safety tests,
not by a proof for all indices.

\section{A certified map of variants of Gödel's ontological argument}
\label{sec:ontological-variant-map}

\subsection*{Setting}

Gödel's ontological argument, in Scott's version, uses a second-order predicate $P$ (``positive'')
and five axioms: A1 (exactly one of a property and its negation is positive; split here into
A1a, ``not both'', and A1b, ``at least one''), A2 (positivity is closed under necessary entailment),
A3 ($P(G)$, where $G$, ``God-like'', means having every positive property), A4 (positive
properties are necessarily positive) and A5 (necessary existence is positive). The conclusion is
that a God-like being exists, $\exists x\,G(x)$, and necessarily exists, $\Box\exists x\,G(x)$.
A well-known side effect is \emph{modal collapse}: everything true is necessarily true.

Many variants and weakenings are studied, mostly one variant at a time with an automated
theorem prover \cite{benzmueller2025notes,benzmueller2026lean,benzmueller2020simplified,benzmueller2019ultrafilters}. This finding maps the space systematically. We take every subset
of each variant's axioms and the nine frame classes K, D, T, B, K4, KB, KD45, S4 and S5. We
vary the semantics: all intensional properties, rigid properties or extensional properties
(Fitting \cite{fitting2002types}), and constant or varying (actualist) domains. Cell by cell we
decide whether the axioms are consistent, whether they prove the conclusion and whether they
force collapse.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Scott, all properties} (constant or varying domains): the God-like being exists exactly when the subset contains A1a, A2, A3 and A5 and the frames are B, KB or S5; necessary existence also holds on KD45. Collapse holds exactly when the subset contains A1a, A2 and A5, on the same frames. A1b and A4 are never needed. With A1, A2 and A5 alone, every symmetric frame is forced to consist of isolated worlds.
\item \textbf{Scott, rigid properties:} with constant domains no consistent cell forces collapse, and the conclusion holds on every frame exactly when the subset contains A1, A2 and A3, or A1, A3 and A5 (with Scott's essence). With varying domains existence needs A1a, A2, A3 and A5 on reflexive frames (T, B, S4, S5), or A4 as well on KB; with A4 added, necessary existence holds on every frame.
\item \textbf{Anderson's variant} (with $H = P(G_A)$): existence needs A1a, A2 and H on B, KB or S5, and on KD45 only with A4 or A1b, and only with constant domains. A5 follows from A2 and H. Adding A1b, the direction Anderson removed, brings collapse back on B and S5, and on KB only together with A4.
\item \textbf{Fitting's extensional variant} ($H = P(G)$, $N = P(NE)$): with constant domains no cell forces collapse, and the conclusion holds exactly for supersets of \{A1a, A2, H\} or \{A1a, H, N\}. With varying domains existence needs all of \{A1a, A2, H, N\} and reflexive frames (T, B, S4, S5); necessary existence also holds on KD45.
\item \textbf{Benzmüller's simplified variant} (no positive empty property, closure under entailment, closure under arbitrary meets): every cell is consistent and none collapses; the conclusion holds exactly for the full axiom set with closure under local entailment, and never with closure under necessary entailment alone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Each of these variants has been studied before, and many single cells of the map are known: the
dependence of collapse on symmetry, the sufficiency of KB, the redundancy of A5 in Anderson's
variant, the absence of collapse in Anderson's, Fitting's and the simplified variant
\cite{benzmueller2025notes,benzmueller2026lean,benzmueller2017hajek,benzmueller2020simplified}.
What we did not find is the complete picture: for each variant, exactly which axiom subsets and
frame conditions give the conclusion or collapse, and the minimal sets. The map shows, for
example, that in Scott's version neither A1b nor A4 ever matters, and that with rigid
properties and constant domains collapse disappears altogether.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

axiom sets, semantic choices and nine frame classes; other variants (other definitions of

\subsection*{Discussion}

The map is a refinement of work that is largely known, not a new argument. Its value is
completeness: for each variant, every subset of the axioms and every listed frame class is decided
with a certificate, so the minimal sets and the frame boundaries can be read off rather than
inferred from separate studies. Several entries agree with results already established with
other provers \cite{benzmueller2025notes,benzmueller2026lean,benzmueller2017hajek}, which we take
as a check on our encoding rather than as findings.

The map covers only the stated choices: a shallow embedding of higher-order modal logic, Scott's
and Gödel's definitions of essence, the listed variants, nine frame classes and two kinds of
domain. Other definitions of essence, other readings of positivity (for example world-relative
and rigid mixtures), Gödel's conjunction axiom in all slices \cite{benzmueller2026ultrafilters}, and
logics beyond these frame classes are outside it. A cell decided here can therefore change under
a different encoding, and claims of minimality hold relative to the listed axioms.

What a reader can take away is where the strength of each variant sits: in Scott's version the
weight lies on A1a, A2, A3 and A5 together with symmetry, while A1b and A4 are idle; in
Anderson's version the dropped complement direction is exactly the switch for collapse; and in
the rigid and simplified readings, existence holds without collapse. Natural next steps are the
open cells the run left (mixed conjunction principles in some slices) and other definitions of
essence.

\section{A census of modal agents in the prisoner's dilemma}
\label{sec:modal-agent-census}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot prisoner's dilemma, and each can read the other's source code.
Following Barasz et al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved
about the opponent, written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL: $p$ means ``the
opponent cooperates with me'', $q$ ``the opponent cooperates with DefectBot''. We take the language
L of all formulas built from $p$, $q$, $\bot$, $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\Box$ with every atom
under a box, measure size as the number of nodes, and identify agents that behave identically
against every opponent. Payoffs are the standard 5 (temptation), 3 (mutual cooperation), 1
(mutual defection), 0 (sucker). An earlier finding on this site describes the self-defecting
families found in this census; this one reports the census itself.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Counts.} Up to size 9 there are 2815 behaviourally distinct agents (new per size 1 to 9: 1, 4, 8, 12, 31, 98, 255, 646, 1760), and every pairwise outcome is solved exactly.
\item \textbf{No evolutionarily stable agent.} Up to size 9, 180 of the 1335 observational classes are symmetric Nash equilibria, but none is an ESS. Every self-defecting equilibrium is invaded by FairBot, an argument that holds for every modal agent, not only those in the census. Under replicator dynamics from the uniform start, every surviving agent cooperates with itself and the mean payoff tends to 3.
\item \textbf{The round-robin winner never cooperates.} Up to size 9 the top score belongs to agents that act exactly like DefectBot. Up to size 11 the top 16 agents defect against everyone yet beat DefectBot, because more opponents cooperate with them (9863 against 9688); for example $\Box\Box p \wedge \neg\Box(p \vee \Box\bot)$.
\item \textbf{Self-defecting cooperators are almost always exploitable.} Among agents that defect against themselves but cooperate with FairBot and CooperateBot (2, 2, 4, …, 2468, 7032 per size 4 to 14), only 3, 19 and 75 at sizes 12, 13 and 14 are unexploitable by every modal opponent. Each of the others is exploited by one of four simple agents: $\Box\neg p$, $\Box p$, $\Box q$ or $\Box\bot$.
\item \textbf{A threshold law, and where it breaks.} Up to size 14, every self-defecting agent that cooperates with FairBot cooperates with graded FairBots $F_k = \Box^k p$ exactly for $k \le a$ and with graded PrudentBots $P_i$ exactly for $i \ge b$, for some $a < b$. The law fails at size 17: the agent $Y = \Box(\Box\Box\bot \vee (p \wedge (q \vee \neg\Box(q \wedge \Box\Box\Box\bot))))$ cooperates with $F_1$ and $F_k$ for $k \ge 4$, but not with $F_2$ or $F_3$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

The literature on modal agents studies a handful of named agents (FairBot, PrudentBot, CliqueBot
and a few more) \cite{barasz2014robust,lavictoire2014program}. A census makes the space itself
visible: it shows that program equilibrium among modal agents has no evolutionarily stable
point, that a round robin rewards agents that never cooperate, and that self-defecting
cooperators are rarely safe, with four tiny agents enough to expose all the others.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

Every outcome is computed exactly in the height semantics of closed GL fixed points (see
\cite{boolos1993logic}; the link to provability in arithmetic is Solovay's theorem
\cite{solovay1976provability}) and re-checked on large samples by an independent solver; the
datasets are pinned by hash. The counts, equilibria and exploitability results hold for all
agents up to the stated sizes, not in general. The FairBot invasion argument and the
counterexample $Y$ hold in general.

\section{Settling informal claims about modal agents in Chicken}
\label{sec:chicken-informal-claims}

\subsection*{Setting}

Modal agents play one-shot games by asking what can be proved about each other, written with the
provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL \cite{barasz2014robust}. Two informal posts made claims about
specific agents that were never checked. ``Modal Chicken'' \cite{gramstone2015chicken} introduced
CarefulBot and AstuteBot for the game of Chicken, and its comments proposed graded variants:
$C_n = \neg\Box(\Box^n\bot \vee p)$ (``swerve unless the opponent provably swerves'', with proof
strength $n$) and BoldBots $B_n = \Box(\Box^n\bot \vee \neg p)$, the name a commenter gave to the
post's original CarefulBot ($B_0$); here $p$ means ``the opponent swerves against me''. ``Yet More Modal Combat'' \cite{hobson2021modal} proposed, for the prisoner's
dilemma, a bot that defects if a weak system proves the opponent cooperates and otherwise acts like
FairBot with a strong system; in graded form $H_{a,b} = \neg P_a(p) \wedge P_b(p)$ with
$P_a(z) = \Box(\Box^a\bot \vee z)$, $a < b$.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Graded CarefulBots and BoldBots, for all grades:} $C_n$ swerves against $C_m$ exactly when $n \le m$, while $B_n$ swerves against $B_m$ exactly when $n > m$. A CarefulBot against a BoldBot always goes straight while the BoldBot swerves, so the pair always ends at payoffs $(4, 1)$, whatever the grades.
\item \textbf{The named agents:} a certified table of all 64 pairings of SwerveBot, NerveBot, the original CarefulBot $B_0$, $C_0$, AstuteBot as displayed in the post, the FairBots $P_0(p) = \Box p$ and $P_1(p)$, and Hobson's bot $H_{0,1}$ (which cooperates with itself but ends in mutual defection with both FairBots). The original CarefulBot crashes against itself, as a comment pointed out; the displayed AstuteBot swerves against itself, as the post claimed.
\item \textbf{Hobson's bot, for all grades:} $H_{a,b}$ and $H_{c,d}$ end in mutual cooperation exactly when their weak grades agree ($a = c$), and otherwise in mutual defection. Against any FairBot of the form $P_e(p)$ it ends in mutual defection, for every $e$.
\end{itemize}

Informal claims about small agents are easy to make and easy to get wrong; two of the claims in
these posts were already disputed in their comment threads. These results settle them for every
grade at once. They also show that Hobson's bot, proposed as a bot that cooperates with FairBots,
does not cooperate with any graded FairBot of this form.

\section{Three-player modal agents in a public-goods game}
\label{sec:three-player-modal-agents}

\subsection*{Setting}

Modal agents decide what to do by asking what can be proved about the other players, written with
the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL \cite{barasz2014robust}. Mennen \cite{mennen2017modal} set up a
framework for more than two players; here we study three players concretely. The game is a
public-goods game: each player contributes ($a_i = 1$) or not, and earns
$u_i = 2\sum_j a_j - 3a_i$, so contributing costs 1 net but gives each other player 2. An agent sees
$p_1, p_2$ (``opponent $j$ contributes in this game``) and probes $q_1, q_2$ (``opponent $j$
contributes when I am replaced by a defector``). Behaviour is read off in the long run (tail
semantics). An agent is \emph{safe} if it never earns less than either opponent, whoever they are.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The three-player FairBot is safe.} $F = \Box(p_1 \wedge p_2)$ (contribute iff it is provable that both others contribute) never earns less than either opponent against any two modal opponents, contributes with two copies of itself and defects against a defector.
\item \textbf{It is the smallest.} No agent smaller than $F$ (size 4) both cooperates with copies of itself and is safe.
\item \textbf{All small safe cooperators are FairBot-like.} Exactly 66 agents up to size 6 cooperate with themselves and are safe; none of them uses the probes, and for each GL proves that it contributes only if $\Box^3(p_1 \wedge p_2)$. Every other agent of that size fails against some pair of opponents of size at most 3.
\item \textbf{A prudent three-player agent needs the probe.} $E = \Box((p_1 \wedge p_2) \wedge (\Box\bot \vee \neg q_1))$ (size 10) cooperates with itself, is safe, and defects against two unconditional contributors; no agent of size at most 7 has all three properties.
\item \textbf{The definition of the probe matters.} For five basic three-player bots we certify all 125 triples. Changing the probe from ``only I am replaced by a defector'' to ``everyone except the queried opponent is replaced'' changes the outcome of 9 of the 125 triples.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Program equilibrium with modal agents has been worked out almost only for two players. In a
three-player public-goods game the natural FairBot generalisation stays safe, is the smallest safe
cooperator, and is essentially the only kind up to size 6. Exploiting unconditional contributors
requires the probe, as with PrudentBot in two-player games, and the exact definition of the probe
is a real modelling choice, since outcomes change with it.

\section{Modal agents in the game of Chicken}
\label{sec:chicken-modal-agents}

\subsection*{Setting}

Two programs play a one-shot game and each can read the other's source code. Following Barasz et
al. \cite{barasz2014robust}, a \emph{modal agent} decides by asking what can be proved about the opponent,
written with the provability box $\Box$ of the logic GL. Earlier findings on this site study the
prisoner's dilemma; here the game is mostly \textbf{Chicken}: both swerve 3/3, swerve against straight
1/4, both go straight (crash) 0/0. $p$ means ``the opponent swerves against me'', $q$ ``the opponent
swerves against NerveBot'', the bot that never swerves. SwerveBot always swerves; FairBot is
$\Box p$, graded FairBots are $\Box^j p$. The census covers every agent built from $p$, $q$, $\bot$,
$\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$ and $\Box$ (atoms under a box) up to size 10, 7561 behaviourally distinct
agents. Mennen \cite{mennen2017modal} set up modal agents for general games; this finding works out
Chicken concretely.

\subsection*{Result}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Equilibria never crash.} Among the census agents there are 517 symmetric program equilibria, all with mutual swerving, and no equilibrium at all involves a crash. Every asymmetric equilibrium pairs one of the 701 agents that never swerve against anyone in the census with an agent that swerves against it.
\item \textbf{The smallest sensible Chicken agents} (swerve against themselves and against NerveBot, go straight against SwerveBot) have size 3: exactly $\neg\Box p$ and $\neg\Box q$. The first never crashes against any census agent; the second crashes against 755 of them.
\item \textbf{Safe and fair.} Exactly 4 census agents never crash against a census agent, go straight against SwerveBot and swerve together with FairBot, and all 4 never crash against any modal opponent at all. The smallest, of size 9, is the $k=2$ member of a family $Q_k = \Box^k\bot \vee \neg\Box(p \wedge q)$ with exact laws for every $k$: $Q_k$ never crashes, beats SwerveBot, swerves against itself, swerves together with $\Box^j p$ exactly when $j < k$ and with $\Box(\Box^j\bot \vee p)$ exactly when $j + 2 \le k$, and goes straight against $Q_m$ for $m > k$.
\item \textbf{A limit for every two-action game.} If an agent of modal depth $d$ exploits the always-cooperating bot, then graded FairBots $\Box^j p$ with $j \ge d$ end up playing action 2 against it, and the two never settle on mutual action 1. The $Q_k$ family shows the bound is tight.
\item \textbf{Tournaments and evolution.} In a round robin the top Chicken agent is $\neg\Box p \wedge \Box(\neg p \vee \Box p)$, which crashes against itself. Under replicator dynamics the surviving agents are the same 1116 classes in the prisoner's dilemma, Chicken and Stag Hunt, and they form a maximal set of mutual cooperators containing FairBot.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Why it matters}

Modal agents have been studied almost only in the prisoner's dilemma. Chicken punishes mutual
defection (a crash) instead of rewarding it, so the question changes from ``can I avoid being
exploited?'' to ``can I avoid crashing without being a pushover?''. The census answers it
concretely: equilibria never crash, safe agents exist and form a family with exact thresholds, and
a general depth bound limits how such agents can coexist with graded FairBots.

\subsection*{Proved vs checked}

The laws for $Q_k$ and the depth bound are proved for all indices in Lean 4, in the height
semantics of closed GL fixed points (see \cite{boolos1993logic}; the link to provability is Solovay's
theorem \cite{solovay1976provability}). The census results (equilibria, smallest agents, the four safe
agents, round robin, replicator dynamics) hold for all agents up to size 10, computed exactly and
re-checked on samples; the replicator results are numerical (2000 steps from the uniform start).

We believe this result is new. Our prior-art search (2026-10-20) covered:

\begin{itemize}
\item Mennen, ``Modal combat for games other than the prisoner's dilemma'' (LessWrong / Alignment Forum, 2017): framework for modal agents in games with any number of players and actions (player types, ranks, opponent tests naming a target player, an agent to test against and an action); existence and Pareto theorems; no agent families with index laws, no enumeration.
\item Gram\_Stone, ``Modal Chicken'' (LessWrong, 2015) and its comments (AlexMennen, SilentCal): SwerveBot, NerveBot, CarefulBot, AstuteBot; informal claims about graded CarefulBots and BoldBots.
\item Hobson, ``Yet More Modal Combat'' (LessWrong / Alignment Forum, 2021): a two-system PD bot; generalisation to Chicken and more players left open.
\item Critch, Dennis \& Russell, ``Cooperative and uncooperative institution designs: surprises and problems in open-source game theory'' (arXiv 2208.07006): two-player bounded proof-search agents.
\item Todorov, ten Napel \& Müller, ``Parametric Open Source Games'' (arXiv 2606.27068): non-modal parametric agents; Chicken and Stag Hunt thresholds.
\item Barasz et al. 2014 (arXiv 1401.5577); LaVictoire et al. 2014; Critch, parametric bounded Löb (arXiv 1602.04184); Oesterheld 2022 (arXiv 2211.05057); LessWrong / Alignment Forum wiki ``Modal combat''; web searches for modal or provability-based agents in Chicken, Stag Hunt and multiplayer program equilibrium.
\end{itemize}

We found no earlier statement of it, but we can't be certain. If you know of prior work, please open an issue.

\subsection*{Discussion}

The census stops at size 10, and round-robin winners in particular can change as larger agents
are added. The language has a single probe ($q$, how the opponent treats NerveBot); richer probes
would give a larger space. The replicator results describe this population, not modal agents in
general. A natural next step is Stag Hunt, where the round-robin leader found here is unusual, and
a search for threshold families beyond FairBot-like agents there.
